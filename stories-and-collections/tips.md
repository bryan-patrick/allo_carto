# Tips

| ID | Title | Tip |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Introducing your name | "Je m'appelle Léo" means "My name is Léo." French puts "myself" before "call". English puzzles accept either order for this expression. |
| 2 | À with city names | French uses "à" with city names when saying where someone lives. |
| 3 | Dépanneur in Québec | In Québec, "dépanneur" means convenience store. |
| 4 | De l'eau | "De l'eau" is a common French expression meaning some water. You'll see it often when people talk about drinking or having water. |
| 5 | Questions with est-ce que | "Est-ce que" is a phrase that is placed at the beginning of a statement to turn it into a question. |
| 6 | Dans la ville and en ville | "Dans la ville" means in the city, but "en ville" is another common expression that can also mean in the city or urban life. |
| 7 | Negation with ne and pas | "Ne... pas" usually works together to mean "not." With a simple verb, put "ne" before it and "pas" after it: "Je ne suis pas un chat" means "I am not a cat." "Ne" becomes "n’" before a vowel or silent h. Do not translate the two parts as two separate English "nots." "Pas" can also mean "not" in a short phrase without a verb: "Pas moi" means "Not me." In everyday speech, people often leave out "ne" and keep "pas." |
| 8 | Faire for weather and temperature | "Fait" means "makes" or "does." French uses it for weather: "Il fait chaud" means "It is warm," rather than "It makes warm." If "makes" feels confusing here, it is fine to think of "fait" as "is" in weather expressions. |
| 9 | Le matin | "Le matin" is translated more closely to in the morning instead of just the morning. |
| 10 | Le soir | "Le soir" is translated more closely to in the evening instead of just the evening. |
| 11 | Jusqu'au | "Jusqu'au" is an expression that means up to or all the way to. It's formed from "jusque" + "à" + "le". |
| 12 | Faire une sieste | "Faire" means "do" or "make," and "fais" is the form used with "je." French says "faire une sieste," literally "do a nap." English just uses "take": "Je fais une sieste" means "I take a nap." |
| 13 | Du coin | "Du coin" means local or nearby. It does not necessarily refer to a literal street corner. |
| 14 | Entrer et sortir | "Entrer et sortir" means to come and go, literally to enter and exit. |
| 15 | Lui before the verb | "Lui" is an indirect object pronoun meaning to him or to her. It normally appears before the verb. |
| 16 | Pendant que | "Pendant que" means while, but literally translates to during that. For example, "je regarde pendant que je mange" means I watch while I eat, but translates to I watch during that I eat. |
| 17 | Me before the verb | In French, an object pronoun like "me" normally goes before the verb it belongs to. "Il me donne" means "he gives me," but the word order is literally "he me gives." In "Il aime me donner à manger," "me" goes before "donner" because it tells us who receives what he gives. English puts "me" after "give" instead. |
| 18 | Lowercase weekdays | French days of the week are lowercase: "lundi" means Monday. They still take a capital at the start of a sentence. |
| 19 | On foot | "À pied" means "on foot." In this expression, "à" translates as "on" and "pied" means "foot." In other contexts, "à" can mean "to," "at," or "in." |
| 20 | Near a place | "Près de" means "near." The "de" becomes "d’" before "un": "près d’un parc" means "near a park." |
| 21 | Au combines à and le | "Au" combines "à" + "le." It means "to the" when going somewhere and "at the" when saying where something is. |
| 22 | Listen to | "Écouter" means "listen to." French puts the person or sound straight after the verb: "j’écoute mes amis" means "I listen to my friends." |
| 23 | Talking about things in general | French uses "le," "la," or "les" when talking about things in general. "J’aime les jours chauds" means "I like warm days"; English leaves out "the." |
| 24 | Oiseaux and plural x | "Oiseau" means "bird," and "oiseaux" means "birds." Most French nouns add an "s" for the plural, but nouns ending in "-eau" add an "x." The final "x" is silent when the word is said on its own, so "oiseau" and "oiseaux" sound the same. |
| 25 | Grand and gros | "Grand" and "gros" can both mean "big." "Grand" describes height or overall size: "un grand arbre" is a tall or large tree. "Gros" focuses on bulk or volume: "un gros chat" is a big, bulky cat. For a person, "grand" usually means "tall," while "gros" means "fat." |
| 26 | Mes is plural my | French changes "my" to match the noun: "mon arbre" means "my tree," "ma sieste" means "my nap," and "mes amis" means "my friends." Use "mes" before a plural noun, whether it is masculine or feminine. English keeps "my" the same for one thing or several. |
| 27 | Un and une mean a or one | "Un" means "a," "an," or "one" with masculine nouns; "une" does the same with feminine nouns. "Un chat" can mean "a cat" or "one cat." French uses the same word for the article and the number. The context tells you which meaning fits. |
